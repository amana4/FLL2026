# The Problem

## Our problem statement

One or two sentences. Specific enough that you could tell whether it was solved.

> Almost everyone who wants to "save the bees" is thinking of honeybees, which
> are managed livestock. The bees that actually need help are the hundreds of
> native, mostly solitary species living around us — and **nobody, including us,
> knows how many different kinds live in their own backyard.** Because nobody
> measures it, nobody can tell whether anything they do for bees works.

**Weak:** "Pollution is bad for the environment."
**Strong:** "Elderly residents in our town can't read the recycling labels, so 30%
of what they put in recycling bins is contaminated and gets landfilled."

## The distinction the whole project rests on

**More bees is not the goal. More *kinds* of bee is.**

Honeybees live in managed hives, are kept deliberately in large numbers, and in
the Americas are not native. Where they are abundant they can compete with wild
bees for the same flowers. So a garden full of honeybees is not evidence of
success, and counting them as a win would make our results meaningless.

Most native bees are **solitary** — no hive, no honey, one female doing all the
work — and the large majority nest **in the ground**, not in boxes. That single
fact changes what actually helps them, and it is the part most people have never
heard.

If the team can explain this clearly, it is the strongest thing in the
presentation. It is also the easiest thing to get wrong.

## Why it matters

Gardens and lawns add up to an enormous area of land, and unlike a nature reserve
they are under the direct control of ordinary people. If a household change can be
shown to raise the number of native bee species in a garden, that is a change
millions of households could copy.

The gap is measurement. Advice on helping bees is everywhere; evidence that a
particular garden got better is almost nowhere, because the standard scientific
method needs an expert and a microscope.

## How it connects to BIOGLOW

The official brief:

> During the BIOGLOW, teams were challenged to **identify and research a problem
> that puts biodiversity at risk** and design an innovative solution that can
> help.
>
> — BIOGLOW Season Overview

Native bee decline is a biodiversity problem in the most literal sense: the issue
is not the total number of insects but the **number of different species**, and
specialist bees that depend on particular plants disappear first. Losing them
takes the plants that depend on them too. Our project measures diversity directly
and tries to increase it.

## What we don't know yet

Open questions to drive research. These are the ones to put to an expert.

- How many native bee species should we expect in a suburban backyard here?
- Can we tell species apart from photographs at all, or only broader groups?
- Which single change to a garden gives the biggest increase, fastest?
- How much of the year are native bees active where we live? This decides whether
  a before-and-after inside our season is even possible.
- Do we risk *harming* bees with anything we plan to do — a badly built bee hotel,
  for instance?
