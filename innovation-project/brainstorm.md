# Brainstorm

Weeks 1–2. Go wide first, judge later — mixing the two kills good ideas early.

Five minutes each, working alone, then share. **This is worth doing before the
open brainstorm**, because five kids in a room converge fast — usually on bees
or plastic. Assigning layers forces four genuinely different problem spaces,
and guarantees somebody thinks about soil and fungi, which teams always skip.

Translate to where you live: your local park has a canopy, a shrub layer,
ground, and soil. Same question in each — *what's in trouble here, and who'd
notice?*

## Rules for the open brainstorm

1. **Quantity first.** Target 20+ ideas before evaluating any of them.
2. **No criticism during the wide phase.** "Yes, and" not "no, but".
3. **Everyone contributes** — go round the table so the loudest voice doesn't
   set the direction.
4. **Write everything down**, including the silly ones. Silly ideas next to
   sensible ones often produce the good one.

## What makes a good FLL problem

| A good problem is… | Because |
| --- | --- |
| **Specific** | "Bees are declining" is a topic. "Our town's roadside verges are mown before wildflowers seed" is a problem you can solve. |
| **Local** | You can visit it, photograph it, and find someone affected to interview. |
| **Unsolved** | If a perfect solution exists, there's nothing to innovate. |
| **Interesting to you** | Twelve weeks is a long time on something boring. |
| **Testable** | You can build a model or prototype and show it works. |

The strongest projects almost always come from something the team can go and
look at in person.

## Biodiversity angles to spark ideas

Prompts, not answers — the point is to generate your own.

**Habitat**
- Fragmented habitats and wildlife corridors; roads and railways as barriers
- Urban green space: verges, roofs, gardens, cemeteries, school grounds
- Rivers and wetlands; culverts and weirs blocking fish
- Dead wood, hedgerows, field margins

**Species**
- Pollinators beyond honeybees — solitary bees, hoverflies, moths
- Invasive species and how they spread
- Soil life: earthworms, fungi, microbes
- Night-time species and light pollution
- Local species nobody notices or records

**Human systems**
- Farming practices and biodiversity
- What gets planted in public spaces, and who decides
- Mowing and "tidiness" as a cultural habit
- Food supply chains and land use
- Construction and development rules

**Knowledge**
- Citizen science: who records what, and what's missing
- Species identification as a barrier to participation
- Monitoring cost and effort
- Getting data to the people who make decisions

**Attitudes**
- Which species people care about, and which they don't
- Fear of insects, bats, spiders
- What schools teach about local nature
- Making biodiversity visible in daily life
