## What you are looking at

**Bee groups** is the number that matters. Most native bees cannot be named to
species without a microscope and a specialist, so instead we sort photographs into
piles that look different from each other — size, colour, hairiness, stripes, where
they carry pollen. Scientists call these morphospecies, and it is a real published
method.

A garden that gains new *kinds* of bee is a garden that got better. That is the
experiment. [How the counting works](field-protocol.md) explains the rules.

**Honeybees are counted but excluded from the score.** They are kept livestock, not
wild pollinators, and they are not what the project is trying to help. A garden that
gained six honeybees and no new wild types has not succeeded.

## Why a count can be missing

The protocol throws out counts that cannot be compared. A count only happens if it
is warm, bright and not windy, between 10:00 and 15:00, for a timed ten minutes on
the same marked patch. A cold windy morning gives near-zero bees whatever the garden
is like, so counting on one would make the data worse rather than better.

So gaps in this chart are usually the weather, not forgetting.

## What is not shown here

Garden locations, plant names and the written descriptions stay out of this page.
The counts are ours to share; the addresses they were taken at are not.

First names of whoever counted are shown, which matches how the
[after-class notes](../docs/after-class-notes.md) already work.

The photographs are not public either. They are in a restricted Drive folder,
because a garden photo can catch somebody in the background. Ask the coach if you
need them.
