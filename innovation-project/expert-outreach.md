# Expert Outreach

**Start this in week 1.** It's the single most time-sensitive thing in the
season, and it has nothing to do with the robot.

Real conversations with experts are what separates a strong Innovation Project
from a web-search one — judges score original research explicitly. But experts
are busy: a reply can take two or three weeks, and many never reply at all.

> **The maths:** email 10 people in week 1 → maybe 3 reply → maybe 2 will talk
> to you → you have real research by week 4. Email 10 people in week 6 and you
> have nothing before the competition. Send more emails than feels necessary,
> earlier than feels necessary.

## Who to contact for a biodiversity theme

Aim for 10+ initial emails across several categories:

| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Universities | Biology, ecology, environmental science departments; PhD students often reply fastest and have the most time |
| Local government | Council ecology or biodiversity officer, parks department, tree officer |
| Conservation groups | Wildlife trusts, land trusts, Audubon/RSPB-type societies, river/watershed groups |
| Land managers | Nature reserve wardens, national/state park rangers, botanic gardens, arboretums |
| Working professionals | Farmers using regenerative methods, beekeepers, landscape architects, foresters |
| Citizen science | iNaturalist power users, local bird or bat survey groups, BioBlitz organisers |
| Zoos & aquariums | Education officers are used to talking to students and usually say yes |
| Museums | Natural history collections, entomology curators |

**Local beats famous.** A council ecologist who will meet you for 30 minutes is
worth far more than a celebrity scientist who never replies — and a local
problem makes a better project than a global one.

## Email template

Short, specific, easy to say yes to. Students should write and send it
themselves — that's part of what's being judged.

> **Subject:** Student robotics team — 15 minutes on [topic]?
>
> Hello [Name],
>
> We're a *FIRST* LEGO League team of [N] students aged [ages] in [town]. This
> season's challenge is about biodiversity, and we're researching [specific
> topic].
>
> We found your work on [specific thing — be concrete, it shows you looked] and
> hoped you might spare **15 minutes** for a video call or answer a few
> questions by email. We're especially curious about:
>
> - [specific question]
> - [specific question]
>
> We're students, this is unpaid and voluntary, and we're grateful for whatever
> time you can give. Our competition is in early November.
>
> Thank you,
> [Names], [Team name]

**What makes this work:** a named ask ("15 minutes"), specific questions, and
evidence you read their work. A generic "can you help with our project?" gets
deleted.

## Following up

One polite follow-up after **7–10 days** is appropriate — not pushy, and it
often works. People are busy, not uninterested. After one follow-up with no
reply, move on.

## Before the call

- [ ] Questions written down and agreed in advance
- [ ] Someone assigned to take notes, someone to ask
- [ ] Ask permission before recording
- [ ] Know what you'll do with their answer

## After the call

- [ ] Write it up in [`research.md`](research.md) the same day
- [ ] **Send a thank-you email** — always, within 24 hours
- [ ] Ask if they'd be willing to look at the solution later. This is how you
      get the feedback that scores the Iterate criterion.
- [ ] Add the outcome to the tracker above
