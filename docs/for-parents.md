# For Parents

Everything you need to know in one page. Written for the five families, and worth
reading once even if your child is the one doing all the work.

## What FLL Challenge actually is

It is not only a robotics competition. That surprises most people, and it is the
single most useful thing to understand.

Teams are judged in **four equally weighted areas**:

| Area | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Robot Game** | The robot scores points on a table in 2.5-minute matches |
| **Robot Design** | Judges ask the students how they built and programmed it |
| **Innovation Project** | The team finds a real biodiversity problem, designs a solution, and shares it with people who could use it |
| **Core Values** | How they work together — judged everywhere, all day |

So the robot is **one quarter**. If your child comes home talking about interviewing
a council ecologist rather than building, that is the plan working, not a distraction
from it.

The season theme is **BIOGLOW** and the subject is biodiversity.

| Sessions | 26, about 39 hours of table time |
| Homework | 15 to 30 minutes between meetings |
| The event | One full Saturday, most of the day |

Homework is deliberately small. If it is taking your child longer than half an hour,
tell the coach and we will cut it — a burnt-out nine-year-old in October is a worse
outcome than an unfinished task.

**Attendance matters more than talent.** Turning up twice a week for thirteen weeks
is the best predictor of a good season, and a missing student holds up whichever
workstream they were on.

## What you must not do

This is the part worth reading twice, because helping in the wrong way costs real
points.

The official rule is that **"all work presented at events is the work of the children
on the team"**, and both judging rubrics specifically reward evidence that all five
students contributed. Judges test this by choosing which child they question. An
adult-built robot that a child cannot explain scores worse than a wobbly one they
designed themselves.

The participation rules also say adults "interfering with, excessively instructing,
prompting, or heckling a team" **may be asked to leave**.

So please do not build attachments, type into the coding app, write the expert
emails, rewrite the presentation, or answer a judge's question for them — even to
help them out of a stuck moment.

## What genuinely helps

1. **Introductions to experts.** This is the most valuable thing an adult can do.
   The team needs to interview real people about biodiversity, and you have a network
   your child does not. A university contact, someone at the council, a beekeeper, a
   ranger — making the introduction is not doing their work.
2. **Being an awkward mock judge** in October. Ask them hard questions and let them
   struggle.
3. **Getting them there.** Twice a week, on time.
4. **Printing, charging, feeding, driving.**
5. **Noticing when someone did something well.** Core Values is a quarter of the
   score and it is mostly about how they treat each other.

## What competition day looks like

One Saturday, most of the day. Roughly:

- **Arrive and check in.** Find the pit table, set up, find the practice tables.
- **Robot inspection**, early rather than late.
- **One judging session**, about 30 minutes, all five students in a room with judges.
  Two minutes to set up, five minutes presenting the project, five minutes of
  questions, five minutes explaining the robot, five more questions, then feedback.
- **Three matches** of 2.5 minutes each, spread through the day. Only the best score
  counts, so a bad first match is genuinely fine.
- **Awards** at the end.

There is a lot of waiting. Bring something to do, water, snacks and lunch.

Parents watch matches from the stands, not at the rail. Judging rooms are usually
students and judges only.

## Awards, and expectations

There are eleven awards. The big one, Champion's, is decided by adding up the team's
**rank** in each of the four areas, lowest total winning — so consistency beats
brilliance in one area.

Three awards are aimed at teams in their first year: **Breakthrough** (significant
progress in confidence and capability), **Rising All-Star** (a team judges expect
great things from), and **Motivate** (team building, spirit and enthusiasm). Those
are realistic for us, and worth mentioning to your child so first place is not the
only thing that counts.

There is also a **Coach/Mentor Award** that the students themselves nominate for.

## Photos and privacy

The team has a public website built from our notes. It deliberately contains **no
photos of students and no full names** — the roster with names is kept out of it.

Photos of the team go in the shared Google Drive folder, not on the website and not
in the code repository, because those keep permanent history.

If you would rather your child not appear in photographs at all, just tell the coach.
It is easy to accommodate and nobody needs a reason.
