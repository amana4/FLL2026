# FIRST's 12 Sessions, Mapped onto Our 26

FIRST ships a complete 12-session curriculum: a coach's Team Meeting Guide, a matching
student Engineering Notebook, and 13 ready-to-run slide decks. Our 26-meeting plan was
written before any of that was read, so this page reconciles the two.

The good news first. FIRST sizes a session at "**two hours**", 12 sessions, so about
24 hours. We have 26 sessions of 90 minutes, which is **39 hours**. We have more time
than the official plan, not less — the earlier worry about a tight schedule was
misplaced.

The coach guide even anticipates our split:

> "It may take two hours to complete the tasks in a session. If needed, split sessions
> into two separate meetings."

Which is exactly what twice-weekly 90-minute meetings do.

### Where we differ, and whether it matters

**We start the expert outreach far earlier.** FIRST identifies the problem in session
3 and researches in 4 and 5. We email experts in week 1. Ours is better for a
13-week season, because replies take two to three weeks and the rubric wants multiple
people.

**FIRST picks the project problem earlier than we do.** Their session 3, roughly our
M5–M6; we choose at M7. Worth considering moving ours earlier, since everything
downstream waits on it.

**We do not schedule the SPIKE Training Camps at all.** That is a real gap — see
[`spike-lessons.md`](../code/spike-lessons.md). They teach precisely the skills
`my-blocks.md` is trying to invent from scratch.

**Our plan is heavier on repetition.** FIRST has two sessions of iteration; we have
six meetings. That is where our extra 15 hours went, deliberately.

There is also a **Pre-Season Checkpoint** (guide page 8) which is worth reading even
now, retrospectively. Its ten items include confirming registration, sorting the set
into trays, charging the controller and completing updates, reading both guides,
looking over the judging rubrics, and browsing the Multimedia Resources list.

## Official coaching advice worth stealing

Verbatim from the Team Meeting Guide, and more concrete than our own equivalents:

**On choosing missions** (p14) — this is better than what
[`strategy.md`](../robot-game/strategy.md) said, so it has been added there:

> "Look for missions that: Use basic robot skills like push, pull, or lift. Have
> models close to a launch area. Involve navigation with line detection. Have easy
> access to home."

**On iterating** (p21): "When the team is improving the robot or attachments, tell
them to change one thing at a time."

**On design** (p23): "Remind teams that simple, repeatable designs often outperform
complicated ones."

**On strategy** (p21): "When the team is developing their mission strategy, have them
determine which launch area will be the starting position."

**On attachments** (p17): "If an attachment is needed for a mission, you could keep it
in a plastic bag labeled with the mission number."

**On backups** (p26): "The team should also have a backup of their programs on an
external drive like a USB stick or online storage."

**On judging** (p28) — this one corrected our judging prep: "Plan to talk about your
robot design and game strategy **without the robot game field**."

**On prototypes** (p20): "A prototype does not need to be functional when it's
presented to judges. The team should be able to describe in detail how it would work."

**On the presentation** (p27): "The presentation can have a slideshow, poster, play,
or even a skit. Props can be used, such as costumes, shirts, or hats."

**On practice tables** (p14): "At the robot game matches, two robot game tables will be
set up next to each other. However, during the sessions, you can work with a single
robot game table."

## The official worksheets

The student notebook contains named worksheets that judges recognise, because FIRST
wrote them. Worth using rather than inventing our own equivalents.

| Worksheet | Page | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Team Progress | 8 | Three checkpoints: "We will use Core Values to…", "So far, I have learned…", "I am proud of my team because we…" |
| Pseudocode Worksheet | 28 | Mission name and number, moves 1 to 10, and a **Robot Path Diagram** box |
| Innovation Project Planning | 29 | Five boxes, each with the session it is due in |
| Robot Design Notes | 30 | Mission, attempted, notes, plus attachment and program notes |
| Technology Connections | 31 | Four real-world tech areas mapped to the theme |

The **Pseudocode Worksheet** pairs with our
[field map](../robot-game/field-map.md) and the printable wireframe grid: plan the
route on the grid, write the moves on the worksheet.

The **Innovation Project Planning** page carries FIRST's own deadlines: problem
statement by session 3, research by 5, solution planning by 6, feedback by 8, summary
before the event. Mapped to our meetings that is roughly M6, M10, M12, M16 and M23 —
slightly ahead of our current plan, which is worth knowing.

## The slide decks

Thirteen decks, one per session plus an intro, each with "I can…" learning objectives,
key vocabulary, an introduction, tasks, reflection, sharing and clean-up. Linked
individually from
[official materials](official-materials/README.md).

Even if you are not following the official structure, the decks are useful for two
things: the **"I can…" objectives** give each meeting a one-line purpose, and the
**key vocabulary** per session is a ready-made glossary drip-feed. Several of FIRST's
definitions are tighter than ours and have been folded into
[`glossary.md`](glossary.md).

## What to actually do with this page

Read the coach guide properly — it is 32 pages and genuinely good. Then, before each
of our meetings, glance at the matching official session and its slide deck. Anything
useful, take. Our plan is the schedule; theirs is the curriculum.
